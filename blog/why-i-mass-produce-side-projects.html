Why I Mass Produce Side Projects

On the art of starting many things and finishing some of them.

productivity philosophy side-projects

I have a confession: my GitHub is a graveyard. Rows upon rows of repositories, each one a dream that burned bright for a weekend, maybe two, before fading into the void of "I'll finish this later."

Some people would call this a problem. I used to think it was too. Every abandoned project felt like a failure, a testament to my inability to commit, to follow through, to finish what I started.

But somewhere along the way, I stopped seeing it that way.

// the myth of completion

We're taught that finishing things is what matters. Ship it. Deploy it. Get it to production. And sure, there's truth in that—you can't learn certain lessons until you've seen something through to the end.

But here's the thing nobody talks about: most ideas aren't worth finishing. Not because they're bad, but because their value was in the exploration, not the destination.

"The value of an idea lies in the using of it." — Thomas Edison

Edison was right, but I'd add a corollary: sometimes the "using" of an idea means extracting what you need from it and moving on. The half-built trading bot taught me about API rate limits and async patterns. The abandoned game prototype taught me about ECS architecture. The unfinished ML project gave me intuition about embeddings that I still use today.

// the compound effect of curiosity

Every side project, finished or not, deposits something into your mental bank:

  • A new library you now know exists
  • A pattern you'll recognize next time
  • A mistake you won't make again
  • A connection between domains you didn't see before

These deposits compound. The person with 50 abandoned projects has explored 50 different problem spaces. They've touched 50 different tech stacks. They've hit 50 different walls and figured out how to get around—or through—each one.

// when to let go

The skill isn't in finishing everything. It's in knowing when to finish and when to walk away. Here's my heuristic:

  • Walk away when you've learned what you came to learn
  • Walk away when the excitement is gone and there's no external accountability
  • Push through when you're past the "hard middle" and can see the end
  • Push through when someone else is depending on it
  • Push through when completing it would unlock new opportunities

The key is being honest with yourself about which category you're in.

// the ones that matter

Not everything should be abandoned. Some projects deserve your full commitment. These are the ones where:

  • The problem genuinely matters to you
  • You're building something you'll actually use
  • Completion would meaningfully change your trajectory

For these, yes—push through the boring parts. Grind through the bugs. Ship it even when it's not perfect. Because the lessons from finishing are different from the lessons from exploring.

// embrace the graveyard

So here's my advice: stop feeling guilty about your unfinished projects. They're not failures. They're research. They're reconnaissance missions into territory you might revisit someday—or might not.

The developer with a graveyard of side projects is a developer who has been curious, who has explored, who has followed their interests wherever they led. That's not a bug. That's a feature.

Now if you'll excuse me, I have another side project to start.
